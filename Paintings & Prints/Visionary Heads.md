## On seeing the visionary sitter

While drawing at Varley's, when the head he was sketching slipped from him:

> I can't go on, — it is gone! I must wait till it returns.

> It has moved. The mouth is gone.

> He frowns; he is displeased with my portrait of him.

When Varley questioned a likeness:

> Oh, it's all right! it _must_ be right: I saw it so.

*— as reported in Gilchrist, Life of William Blake, 1880, vol. I, ch. 28, p. 372.*

## On the Ghost of a Flea

![[visionary-heads-ghost_of_a_flea.jpg|Head of the Ghost of a Flea]]

When Varley asked Blake whether he could draw the spirit's likeness:

> I see him now before me.

And when Varley pressed him on what the creature had said:

> All fleas were inhabited by the souls of such men as were by nature blood-thirsty to excess, and were therefore providentially confined to the size and form of insects; otherwise, were he himself, for instance, the size of a horse, he would depopulate a great portion of the country.

*— as reported by John Varley, A Treatise on Zodiacal Physiognomy (1828), quoted in Gilchrist, Life, 1880, vol. I, ch. 28, pp. 373–374.*

## On the act of vision, to Henry Crabb Robinson

In conversations recorded by Robinson in his diary, beginning 10 December 1825, Blake spoke of his paintings as being what he had seen in his visions — and said, repeatedly, in the ordinary tone of everyday speech:

> The Spirit told me.

> I write when commanded by the spirits.

> I have conversed with the Spiritual Sun. I saw him on Primrose-hill.

The Spiritual Sun itself put the question to him, as Robinson records it:

> He said, "Do you take me for the Greek Apollo?" "No," I said, "that (pointing to the sky) that is the Greek Apollo. He is Satan."

*— as reported in Henry Crabb Robinson, Diary, 10 December 1825 and after (Sadler ed., 1869).*

## From the Annotations on Reynolds

On the nature of vision, in Blake's marginal notes to Sir Joshua Reynolds's *Discourses*:

> The man who never in his mind and thoughts travelled to heaven is no artist.

> Vision is determinate and perfect, and he copies _that_ without fatigue.

*— Blake's Annotations on Reynolds, c. 1798–1809 (Ellis & Yeats, eds., The Works of William Blake, vol. II, Bernard Quaritch, 1893, pp. 318–329).*

## On the historical sitters

Varley's notes and Gilchrist's narration record dozens of figures Blake saw and drew during the night sittings — among them Wat Tyler, the Black Prince, Caractacus, William Wallace, Edward I, Old Tom Parr in his youth, Solomon, Pindar, Corinna the Theban, Lais the Courtesan, the man who built the pyramids, and the man who taught Mr. Blake painting in his dreams.

## On the reality of what he saw

To Robinson, on whether his visions belonged to the same order as the things of common day:

> We are all coexistent with God; members of the Divine body.

And on his own identity within that body:

> I was Socrates — a sort of brother. I must have had conversations with him. So I had with Jesus Christ. I have an obscure recollection of having been with both of them.

*— as reported in Henry Crabb Robinson, Diary, 10 December 1825 (Sadler ed., 1869).*
